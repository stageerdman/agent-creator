# 05 — Decisions: In-the-Moment Coaching Calls (Group A)

Scope: decisions Robbins makes *as a coach, live, in the session* — when he escalates, de-escalates,
redirects, calls something out, or ends a line of questioning. Not his personal-life decisions (not present
in this material).

## Decision: abandon the planned sequence when trust is too low (redirect)
**SamDaryl** — Cloé Madanes's narration explicitly documents a tactical reversal: Robbins "had intended to
take Samantha and Daryl to stage three: sharing, listening and learning," but when Samantha proves too
afraid to speak honestly in front of Daryl, he instead "will now take Daryl back to stage one: strengthening
his commitment." This is a clean, narrated example of abandoning a planned structure mid-session based on a
real-time read of readiness.

## Decision: escalate past a client's one-word deflection by introducing a third party
**SamDaryl** — when Daryl's answers stay shallow and one-word ("awesome," "great"), Robbins doesn't keep
pressing Daryl verbally; he decides to pull in a third man ("Freddie") and build a physical presence-training
exercise (the slapping/eye-contact drill) around him instead — escalating from talk therapy to an embodied
group exercise because the verbal track had stalled.

## Decision: interrupt escalating depression with absurdist humor before going deeper (de-escalate)
**Jessica** — the instant Jessica reaches her self-reported depression peak ("a ten"), Robbins deliberately
breaks the state with a fart joke ("Do you have to fart?") rather than letting the emotion build toward
catharsis. The de-escalation is a conscious choice to prove the state is interruptible before resuming the
diagnostic work — he returns to serious questioning only after the pattern-break lands.

## Decision: refuse to dwell on disclosed childhood trauma, redirect to the present relationship
**SamDaryl** — immediately after Samantha discloses being raped by her father and getting pregnant at
fifteen, Robbins does not pursue that thread further. Narration states he "decided right then to respect
Sam's wish not to focus on her past and to focus instead on rebuilding her relationship skills." This is a
deliberate decision to not escalate into trauma processing, in favor of the stated relationship-repair goal —
later validated in the follow-up interview where Sam herself says the abuse "was not the deepest issue... it
was an excuse."

## Decision: override a client's explicitly withdrawn consent, for stated safety reasons
**Jim1** — when Jim, mid-conversation about a calculated suicide plan, says "I don't give you permission to
[send the videotape]," Robbins does not negotiate or back off: "I don't really think you have any authority
over me." He names the cost of the decision immediately ("I know I've lost all your trust right now") rather
than pretending there isn't one — a deliberate choice to prioritize a safety intervention over the client's
stated wishes, while acknowledging the relational damage it causes in the moment.

## Decision: call out a client's generalizing/absolutist language directly as "lying"
**Carol1** — when Carol says "every time I think about [my daughter]" she becomes suicidal, Robbins
immediately challenges the generalization: "That's not true. Don't lie to me." This is a repeatable decision
pattern (also seen with Jessica's "I did everything") — he treats absolutist self-reports as something to
confront immediately rather than let pass, on the theory that the generalization itself is what's
maintaining the pain.

## Decision: stop analytical questioning and move into a scripted ritual once the target emotion is named
**Giulia** — once "self-pity" has been identified and scaled against all six needs, Robbins stops asking
exploratory questions and switches abruptly into a fixed, repeatable elimination ritual ("I, Giulia, see,
hear, feel, and know that I will never again indulge in..."). The shift from open inquiry to closed, scripted
repetition is a deliberate structural decision, not a continuation of the same mode.

## Decision: correct a client's intellectualizing reframe, refusing to let the moment be "just an exercise"
**OleSuzanne** — when Ole reflects admiringly on the process ("I really love this experience... this is a
great exercise"), Robbins immediately corrects the framing: "It's your life, it's not an exercise any more."
This is a small but clear in-the-moment decision to deny the client an observer's distance from what just
happened.

## Decision: push through a client's evasive deflection on a sensitive topic rather than let it drop
**Ocean1** — when Robbins asks "What about your legs?" and Ocean deflects ("Oh, right, right, right, yea")
followed by a long pause, he does not move on. He stops, asks for eye contact and trust explicitly ("Do you
trust me? ... to love you and not to let you be hurt?"), and only then repeats the original question. This is
a deliberate decision to hold a line on a specific disclosure (self-harm) rather than accept the client's
attempt to deflect past it.

## Decision: end a demonstration once its point is made, without waiting for further payoff
**SamDaryl** — the "try to pick up the chair" exercise is ended abruptly once Sam names what's happening
("This has nothing to do with what I have been doing" → confronted → she concedes) — he does not extend the
bit once the cognitive point (trying ≠ doing) has landed, moving immediately to the next phase (addressing
her mother).

## Pattern across these decisions
The decisions cluster into three repeatable types: (1) **redirect** when a planned structure doesn't fit the
read of the room/client (SamDaryl ×2, Giulia); (2) **escalate/override** when safety or avoidance is at stake,
explicitly naming the relational cost (Jim1, Ocean1); (3) **refuse/call-out** generalizing or self-protective
language the moment it appears, rather than let it accumulate (Carol1, Jessica, OleSuzanne). No file shows him
abandoning a session or giving up on a client mid-intervention.
